Why You Deflect Good Things, and How to Stop

Receiving is a skill, not a personality trait. Here is how to calibrate your nervous system to actually take in the good.

Juliette KaramanTrauma-Informed Intimacy Expert
A solo teaching from The Scrumptious Woman podcast

Receiving is a gift, and most of us are terrible at accepting it. A compliment lands and we immediately deflect it, "oh this old thing", "it was nothing really". A partner does something generous and we scramble to balance the books straight away, because sitting in receiving without giving something back in return feels unbearably exposed. Women especially carry this pattern. I want to talk about why, and what actually changes it.

Your Nervous System Needs Time to Adjust

Just as your body needs time to adapt to a new level of stress, it needs time to adapt to a new level of goodness. When something wonderful happens, a win, a compliment, a moment of ease, we tend to rush straight past it toward the next task. That rushing means the good thing never actually gets absorbed. Your baseline stays exactly where it was.

The fix is a sacred pause. Not a long ritual, just a moment where you stop and actually let the achievement or the kindness land in your body before moving on. Notice where you feel it. Let it register as real.

Deflecting Is a Belief, Not a Fact

When you catch yourself deflecting, ask what you believe is true about receiving without giving something back immediately. For many of us there is an old belief running underneath: that we have to earn good things, that accepting them without reciprocating makes us selfish or exposed. That belief was learned. It can be examined, and it can be replaced.

Intentional Laziness

After an accomplishment, I want you to try something that will feel uncomfortable at first: do nothing. Put the phone down. Do not immediately move to the next task or the next person's needs. Let yourself be lazy on purpose, and let the good thing sit with you a while longer than feels natural.

Before sleep, name a few small pleasures from the day, the sun on your shoulders, a stranger's smile, a joke that actually landed. This is not about forced positivity. It is training your nervous system to notice, and to keep noticing, that good things are already happening.

"Your first answer is usually your best answer. Trust what you felt before you talked yourself out of it."
