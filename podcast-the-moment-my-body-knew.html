The Moment My Body Knew My Marriage Was Over

A marriage rarely ends on the argument. This is the story of the morning my body knew, and of the trauma it had been carrying for me all along.

A personal solo episode from The Scrumptious Woman podcast

The morning I decided to leave my marriage, my body did something I did not expect. The evening before, it felt light. Not empty. Light. The kind of light that comes after you have carried something so long you forgot it was on your shoulders, and then it lifts and your chest can finally take a full exhale. We divorced over a decade ago, and we are still completely a family, all four of our children and both of us around one table at Christmas. People ask, how did you manage this?

The Kitchen, The Boy, The Argument

Our eldest, Hani, was about ten. His friends were sitting exams for boarding schools, and he came to me, serious and anxious, asking to at least try. I found it so brave. I told him, tell his father exactly the way he had told me. When his father came back from a trip, Hani asked, and I stood behind him beaming. There was no unkindness meant in the reply, no thought behind it, only: no, that is not what I had in mind for you. Hani's face did something I will never forget. Quiet defeat. The kind a child learns in real time when asking does not work.

That night we had a huge argument, and at some point he said, maybe we should get divorced. I did not sleep. My body shook things off all night. In the morning I came to him and said, I think that is what we need to do. That was the moment the weight shifted off my shoulders. I did not understand then why that argument, of all of them, was the one. I know now.

The Trauma My Body Was Carrying

Underneath the marriage was something I was not yet carrying consciously, because my body was carrying it for me. I was seven. A relative we called uncle, always the life of the party, gave hugs that went on slightly too long, and his hand went where it should not have. As a girl of seven, I only had the confusion of loving someone and feeling wrong in his arms. When I told my mother, she said, that is just what uncles do.

Stay with that sentence the way I have had to. It did not just fail to protect me. It told a seven-year-old that her body's clearest signal, this is wrong, could be overridden by a trusted adult, that love and violation could live in the same gesture, and that she could not trust the difference. That did not stay in childhood. It kept me slightly braced, slightly gone, even when I was fully there. You cannot be fully met by someone when a part of you is guarded.

Touch Base: A Two-Minute Practice

This is the practice I teach every client, and it takes about two minutes. Press your thumb and forefinger toward each other, but keep them an inch or so apart. Focus on the space between them. You may feel heat build up there, or nothing, which is also perfect. Take a breath in through your nose, out through your mouth, and notice where it lands.

Now let the top finger slowly touch your thumb. Let it land. Then move it even slower, about half the speed you think, so you feel every single ridge. Say it out loud: I am safe. I am safe here. Do this only where you actually are safe, no one behind you, nothing creeping up. Practise it twice a day for 21 or 31 days. It uses a meridian point and a kinesthetic anchor. After 31 days, you can simply tap your fingers together and begin to feel safe.

She Left Long Before She Said a Word

When we finally saw a counsellor, he said something I have thought about ever since, especially working with couples: she has already climbed the mountain and started down the other side, and you are still running behind, trying to catch up. That is the line for every man in my DMs right now.

An argument has never once ended a marriage. It is the accumulation. A reach that lands wrong. She goes still, he reads the stillness as rejection and stops reaching, she reads the stopping as proof she was right to go still. Now neither of you is reaching, and nobody ever named it. By the time you notice something is wrong, she has usually already grieved the marriage on her own. We went to the lawyers having already agreed everything ourselves, because we still deeply loved each other, and we said out loud that we always would. Divorce did not have to mean the end of our love, only the end of that contract.

What Actually Rebuilds a Relationship

I do not teach theory, I teach practice, because I have lived it. Date mornings, protected time once a week, nothing allowed to interrupt. Fifteen minutes a week with a communication and intimacy card: one reads the question, the other answers fully, without being fixed, interrupted, reassured, or pushed back, then you switch. Non-sexual touch offered with curiosity and no destination, week after week. There is an entire nervous system sitting between you with years of learned safety that needs to unlearn. A couple came to me who had not had sex in 23 years. Three months into exactly that structure, they were intimate again.

"If any of this is landing, notice it. If part of you instead says, ah, that is not for me, that is also a signal. Your body is giving you something. We are not always ready, and that is okay."
