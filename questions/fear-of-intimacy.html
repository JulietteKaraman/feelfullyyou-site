How do I overcome fear of intimacy and feel safe opening up?

Answered by Juliette Karaman, intimacy and touch expert.

The short answer

You overcome fear of intimacy by making closeness safe for your body first, in small steps with a clear start and end, and only then opening up to another person. I am Juliette Karaman, an intimacy and touch expert in the UK, and the way in I teach begins alone, with an anchor your body can trust.

When women ask me how to overcome fear of intimacy, I think of a courtyard in the mountains of Lebanon. At a retreat I ran there, I paired the women up for a simple exercise: notice what they see in each other. Value neutral, no judgement.

Afterwards I asked how many had been judging themselves. Hands shot up, and the courtyard filled with giggles. Then I asked whether they had judged their partner. Most said not really. They had been so afraid of doing it wrong that they had barely seen the other person at all.

Fear of intimacy often looks like constant monitoring while someone stands right beside them. It is a body doing its job, and it can be taught a different job.

Why am I afraid of opening up to someone I love?

Because your body learned at some point that being open was not always safe, and it protects you faster than you can think. You can love someone and your body can still stay shut. Those two live together.

I call the first part of it the brace. A hand lands on your back, and before you have decided anything, your body tightens. You override it and let the hand stay. The brace has already happened underneath the thought, and it often goes back to something much earlier than the person standing next to you.

Can I overcome fear of intimacy by just trying harder?

No. A nervous system organised around threat does not open on demand. It opens when the conditions are consistent enough, and trustworthy enough, that the body begins to believe it will not be punished for opening.

At that retreat, the structure of the day was the safety. I told the women exactly what would happen and when they would get their phones back, and every session built on the last. When the brain can anticipate what comes next, the vigilance load drops.

More effort, more candles, more things that bring in sensation can add input to a body that is already guarded. Predictability is what helps first.

How can I overcome my fear of intimacy and feel safe opening up?

Make safety the first step, then open up in layers. In order:

  1. Begin with an anchor that is entirely yours. Touch Base: thumb to forefinger, feel the ridges, say "I am safe right now", and hold for two minutes. Do it somewhere you really are safe, such as your bed or your car.
  2. Ask yourself one question. What do I need right now to feel even more safe? Start with yourself before anyone else is involved.
  3. Choose the container before the conversation. Know when it starts, when it ends and what will happen in between, so your body can anticipate it.
  4. Let the first answer be the safe one. The first few times you answer a deep question, you surface swim. Each repetition goes a little deeper. The question stays the same and your willingness grows.

The Intimacy and Communication Cards were built for step four. One question, asked and answered in layers, with a start and an end.

What happens when it starts to feel safe?

The women found out what they had been holding. After they wrote what they desired on post-it notes and put them on the wall, several of them phoned home. One told her husband she had been assuming for years that she knew what he wanted, and had left no room for him to surprise her. She had not known that until she saw her own desire on the wall.

That is what opening up looks like once it is safe: curiosity coming back before closeness does. I tell the story in full in my essay Creating Safety, and I talk about the brace and Touch Base in the Season 2 episode on why a date night changed nothing, on the podcast.
