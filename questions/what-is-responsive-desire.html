The questions people ask · For women

What is responsive desire, and is it normal?

Answered by Juliette Karaman, intimacy and touch expert.

The short answer

Responsive desire is wanting that arrives after touch or closeness has begun, and yes, it is a normal way for desire to work. Juliette Karaman, an intimacy and touch expert in the UK, works with it by giving the body a clear container first, so it knows what is happening and when it stops, and the wanting can follow the touch.

Say the word structure to most women and something in them goes tight. It sounds like admin. It sounds like the opposite of being wanted.

Say the word dominatrix to the same woman and she leans in.

I used to be a dominatrix, and that room taught me more about responsive desire than almost anything else. Before anything began, two people agreed. What’s on, what’s off, what’s a hard no, how long it lasts. With that container in place, bodies opened faster than in anything else I have worked in.

Then take that home for a second. A bedroom with no edges. No start, no end, no way to say “not now” without it turning into a whole conversation about the state of the relationship. Every reach could be nothing or it could be everything… so the body prepares for everything. Every single time.

What is responsive desire?

Responsive desire is wanting that shows up once something has already started. Touch, closeness or the right setting comes first, and desire builds out of them.

So if you have ever said “I never feel like sex until we start”, you have described responsive desire almost word for word. Its opposite, spontaneous desire, arrives first, out of a thought. I put the two side by side in Responsive vs Spontaneous Desire.

Is responsive desire normal?

Yes, responsive desire is normal, and the research has said so since 2000.

  • Rosemary Basson, 2000. Working from women’s own experience, she drew desire as a circle, where closeness and stimulation can come before the wanting.
  • Emily Nagoski, Come As You Are. She brought it to a wide audience and cites roughly 30% of women and about 5% of men as mainly responsive.
  • Time together. Responsive desire becomes more common after the first six to twelve months of a relationship.
  • The old label. A body that wanted second used to be filed under a desire disorder, and Basson’s model places it as a normal variant.

What is responsive desire in women like after 40?

For many women in their 40s, 50s and 60s, desire moves towards the responsive end, and that shift is expected.

The wanting that used to arrive on its own starts turning up later, after the touch has begun. A lot of women read that as a loss, when what has changed is the order. If that is happening alongside menopause, this answer goes into the body side of it.

How do you work with responsive desire?

Give it a container, so your body knows what is happening before the touch begins. This is the dungeon, scaled all the way down and taken home.

  1. Agree before anything starts. What’s on, what’s off, what’s a no.
  2. Agree how long. Once your body knows how long something lasts, it can relax and stay.
  3. Use a stoplight. Green, keep going. Orange, slow down and take a breath, this is where the growth is. Red, everything stops.
  4. Let a no be heard the first time. That is what lets your body stop preparing for everything.
  5. Curiosity instead of a goal. The touch is going nowhere. Notice what your body does once it knows that.

It sounds hella unjuicy. It is also how your body starts to explore, and once you have done two or three minutes of it you can start expanding the capacity. I teach the whole thing in What a Dungeon Taught Me About Your Bedroom, and I wrote about the no in The Body Does Not Open on Command.

Picture another year of waiting to feel like it before you let anyone near you. Then picture two agreed minutes with a clear end, and what your body does once it knows.
