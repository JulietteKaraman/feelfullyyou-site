The Science

Why every part of The Touch Reset is built the way it is, and the named research it rests on.

“Intimacy doesn't come back through spontaneity. It comes back through structure.”

Touch Base®, the Dyad, the timed container, the Touch Map™, RRRR®, the Full Stop, the Felt Sense: every tool here was built the way it is for a reason. This is that reason, in full.

01 · The Science

Nine areas of nervous system research, one for each stage of how a person moves from threat to genuine openness. Each is named specifically, with the real study or researcher behind it, because every tool below is built directly on one or more of these.

1

Why a command backfires

Directive language, even something as caring as “calm down,” is a form of social information: research on emotions as social signals (Van Kleef's EASI model, 2009) shows that what one person expresses toward another is read as data about the relationship, not neutral instruction. To an already-activated system, an external command reads as pressure from outside, not information about what's actually happening inside. A parent who says “stop crying” to a toddler mid-meltdown is a familiar version of this: the words rarely land, and the crying often gets worse, because the command itself has become one more thing the child's system has to process and defend against.

2

The arousal-valence map

Psychologist James Russell's circumplex model of affect (1980) proposed that emotional states plot on two independent axes, arousal (how activated a system is) and valence (whether the pull is toward something or away from it), rather than sitting on one line from calm to activated. A surgeon daydreaming mid-operation is in the wrong arousal state for the task, but that identical low, unfocused state is exactly right once they're home trying to rest. A client who goes very quiet can be read as calm when the quiet is actually low arousal paired with negative valence, a shutdown, not a settled state, and the two call for entirely different responses.

3

Co-regulation comes before self-regulation

Self-regulation isn't present at birth, it develops through repeated experience of co-regulation: an infant who cannot yet settle itself is organised by proximity to a regulated caregiver's voice, warmth and steadiness. The pattern doesn't end in childhood. In a well-known 2006 study (Coan, Schaefer & Davidson, Psychological Science), married women facing the threat of mild electric shock showed measurably reduced threat-related brain activation simply while holding their husband's hand, before anything was said, strongest in the highest-quality marriages.

4

Interbrain synchrony

EEG hyperscanning, recording two or more people's brain activity at the same time, has found that genuine shared attention produces measurable synchrony between two nervous systems. Researchers including Suzanne Dikker (NYU) and Caroline Szymanski (Max Planck Institute for Human Development) have shown this synchrony correlates with better joint problem-solving, teams literally end up on the same wavelength. The research is specific that this can't be faked: attempts to manufacture synchrony through performance tend to disrupt it rather than produce it.

5

The body's broadcast, and it runs both ways

Physiological state transmits through identifiable channels: facial muscle activity, jaw tension, vocal rhythm, and breath all carry information a nervous system nearby can read below conscious awareness (Stephen Porges named this scanning process neuroception). Breath is the most direct lever, a slow, quiet exhale longer than the inhale engages the vagus nerve within about 60 to 90 seconds. The relationship runs both ways: internal state shapes these signals, and deliberately changing one, the breath, the gaze, a paused hand, shifts the internal state back.

6

Emotion granularity

Psychologist Lisa Feldman Barrett's research on emotional granularity shows that people who describe internal states with real specificity, rather than a broad label like “bad” or “stressed,” show better emotion regulation. Separately, affect-labeling research (Lieberman et al., 2007, Psychological Science) found that putting precise words to an emotional state measurably reduced amygdala activity, but only with real specificity: a vague, distanced label didn't do the same work as a precise, located one. Language that names a fixed identity (“I'm an anxious person”) is associated with a state feeling more permanent, rather than something that can move.

7

Approach-avoidance neurochemistry

The brain's approach and avoidance circuitry run on different neurochemical signatures: approach on dopamine, curiosity, exploration; avoidance on the threat response, which measurably narrows attention while it's active. A person who intellectualises, jokes, or goes flat isn't necessarily choosing to disengage, their system may genuinely have less access to open processing in that moment. What's associated with lowering that response: removing pressure to change, protecting real autonomy, and offering a small, bounded step rather than one large, undefined ask.

8

The collective field

Hyperscanning research extends past pairs to groups: research on emotion regulation broadly (Gross, 1998; Ochsner & Gross, 2005) establishes that regulation is not purely an individual, internal event, it happens in a social context that shapes what's available to a person moment to moment. A nervous system's signals move outward through voice, face, pace and presence into everyone in range, and one strongly regulated, or dysregulated, person can measurably shift what an entire shared field can access.

9

Experience-dependent change

Neuroplasticity, the brain's capacity to reorganise through repeated experience, is experience-dependent: the pathways built through consistent practice become the nervous system's default architecture over time. This is why a two-minute daily practice outperforms an occasional long one, and why naming a sensation once doesn't do what naming it as a repeated habit does. Change compounds through repetition, not through a single big event.

Before you explain a feeling, your body already knows something is happening. This is the vocabulary for that: the words for what is actually going on inside you, before the story arrives to explain it away.

How to use it with a partner: instead of reaching for the story first, start with the body. Before explaining what happened or what it meant, choose the word that feels closest to what's actually happening inside you, right now. This is not a technique to get right, it's a pause, long enough to notice, before the explaining starts.

03 · The Tools

Each one, tied back to the named research above.

Touch Base®

How it works

Thumb to forefinger, a slow breath, “I am safe right now.” Two minutes, daily.

Why it works

A slow, quiet exhale, longer than the inhale, is one of the most direct levers into the vagus nerve, producing a measurable shift in arousal in roughly 60 to 90 seconds (Concept 5). Two minutes isn't a shortened version of something bigger, it's the dose that matches the mechanism. Because neuroplastic change compounds through repetition, not occasional intensity (Concept 9), a daily two-minute practice builds more real capacity over months than an occasional longer reset ever would, the same logic behind why brushing your teeth for two minutes twice a day works and one long scrub once a month doesn't.

Structured Communication: The Dyad

How it works

One prompt, turns, three responses: “Thank you. / Clarify that. / Summarise that.” The switch happens on every “Thank you.”

Why it works

A fixed structure removes the guesswork of how to raise something, so a nervous system isn't managing the threat of an unscripted confrontation while also trying to locate what it's actually feeling (Concept 6, emotion granularity). Shared, structured attention is exactly the condition associated with measurable interbrain synchrony (Concept 4), the mechanism behind two people in genuine back-and-forth ending up on the same wavelength, and structured turns are what make that shared attention possible in the first place.

The Timed Container

How it works

Structured touch dates, 20 to 30 minutes. The timer ends it, not the mood.

Why it works

An open-ended invitation carries an unknown, potentially unlimited cost, and unfamiliar or vulnerable situations are treated as threats by default (Concept 7). A hard start and end make the cost calculable, precisely what's associated with lowering a guarded system's avoidance response. It works the same way a scheduled 20-minute meeting is easier to walk into than an open-ended “let's talk.” The container isn't a limit on desire, it's the condition that makes desire safe to access at all.

The Touch Map™

How it works

One body part. Run through the five pleasure languages as five distinct touches (Touch A, Touch B…). Rate each live on a stoplight: Green, yes, go for it. Orange, maybe, pause or adjust. Red, no touch at all, stop. Done as a self-touch date first, before any partner version.

Why it works

One global question (“did that feel good”) gives almost no usable signal. Isolating one body part into five distinct, individually-rated qualities is emotion granularity (Concept 6) applied to touch itself. The stoplight is a direct, embodied version of the arousal-valence map (Concept 2): Green as pull-toward, Red as pull-away, Orange the real third state most systems collapse into a binary and lose. Doing it alone first means the map is built from one nervous system's own data, before another person's signals are in the room too (Concept 8).

RRRR® (Rapid Release, Rewire, Restore)

How it works

Every experience is broken into four elements: an image, a thought, a body sensation, and an emotion. The body sensation is located using the Felt Sense vocabulary.

Why it works

This takes emotion granularity (Concept 6) further still: four genuinely distinct channels tracked individually, an image and a thought (more cognitive) alongside a body sensation and an emotion (more felt). The Rewire and Restore stages depend on the same experience-dependent change that makes any repeated practice stick (Concept 9): naming a pattern once doesn't rewire it, running the process again each time it surfaces does.

The Full Stop

How it works

Stop. Right there. No touch at all.

Why it works

The functional equivalent of Red on the Touch Map's stoplight, a genuine, trusted stop (Concept 2). It's also what makes the rest of the system trustworthy: approach-avoidance research (Concept 7) is specific that a guarded nervous system needs real autonomy, including a credible exit, before it will risk approaching anything at all. Without a real stop, nothing else here works.

The Felt Sense

How it works

Before the story, the sensation. What is the body doing right now, tight, warm, heavy, open, before any word tries to explain it.

Why it works

A directive aimed at the reasoning brain isn't well received by a system already scanning for threat (Concept 1, and Concept 5's neuroception). Precise, sensation-first language is what actually bridges to the more reflective part of the brain (Concept 6). Sensation first, word second, is the order the research supports.

Why the Order Matters

How it works

Self-touch date and Touch Map, then structured communication, then the couple's own timed, structured touch date. Not the other way round.

Why it works

A guarded system opens through safety without an agenda (Concept 7), and one person's regulation state visibly shifts what a shared moment can access (Concept 8). Doing the self-work first means each person brings a clean, already-mapped signal into the shared moment, instead of two unmapped systems reading each other in real time, exactly the condition that raises perceived risk. The same reason a duet rehearses parts separately before playing together.

04 · Bilateral Stimulation & Discharge

A separate area of research, not about the tools above, about movement itself: eyes side to side, shadow boxing, pillow hitting, cross-body work. Two distinct mechanisms, kept deliberately apart rather than one vague “movement helps” claim, because they're different research bases doing different things.

A

Eyes side to side: bilateral stimulation

Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR), developed by psychologist Francine Shapiro, uses bilateral stimulation, alternating left-right stimulation, classically eye movements, alongside recalled material. It's a genuinely well-evidenced treatment: recognised as a first-line PTSD treatment by major international clinical guidelines, backed by more than 30 randomised controlled trials. The best-supported explanation, still actively researched, not fully settled: the eye movements tax working memory, which measurably reduces how vivid and emotionally intense a recalled memory feels, and is linked to lower amygdala activity while it's happening. A separate, newer hypothesis proposes the alternating stimulation shifts brain activity into a pattern resembling REM sleep's own memory-processing state.

B

Shadow boxing, pillow hitting: discharge

Physiologist and trauma researcher Peter Levine observed that wild animals discharge a mobilised survival response physically, trembling, shaking, running, once a threat passes, which returns the nervous system to baseline. His finding: humans often override that discharge instead of completing it, leaving the physiological activation stored rather than resolved, the basis of his Somatic Experiencing approach. Separately: stress hormones released in a threat response take roughly 60 to 90 minutes to clear from the bloodstream on their own, and deliberate physical movement is the most consistently supported way to help the body register that the threat has passed (popularly known as completing the stress cycle, a framing from Emily and Amelia Nagoski, grounded in the same physiology). The mechanic here: the body was mobilised for a response that had nowhere to go, the movement gives it somewhere to go, and that's what completes it.

None of this was designed from theory first. Every tool here came from years of direct work with real bodies and real couples, then held up against established, named research to understand why it actually works. The practice came first. The science confirmed it.
