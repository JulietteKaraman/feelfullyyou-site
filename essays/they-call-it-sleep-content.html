They Call It Sleep Content

What the body knows that the official record doesn't.

By Juliette Karaman · 19 April 2026

Content note: This essay contains accounts of drug-facilitated rape.

I Had This Filed Under Later

Too raw for early in the sequence, I told myself. Build more trust first. Let people know who you are before you tell them this.

Then CNN published their investigation last month and my phone started filling up. Women sending me the link. Men sending me the link. Former clients. Strangers. People I hadn’t heard from in years.

I cannot file this under Later anymore.

Before I Tell You What CNN Found

I need to tell you something first. Because the order matters.

I was eighteen. My then-boyfriend invited me and a friend to a party in Spain. They spiked our drinks. He invited the men at the party to have their way with us.

I fought.

I fought until I passed out.

I did not consciously remember this during my marriage. I want you to stay with that for a moment, because I know what you might be thinking. How does a woman not remember something like that?

Research on sexual trauma tells us that between 19 and 62 percent of survivors experience partial or total amnesia for the events. The brain, flooded with adrenaline and cortisol at a level that constitutes a genuine threat to the heart and brain, triggers neurobiological survival mechanisms. The emotional circuit cuts off. The memory is stored, but stored differently. Not as narrative. As sensation, fragment, image. Disconnected from conscious recall. It surfaces when conditions are finally safe enough to let it.

My body knew the whole time. It communicated in the only language available to it, in the bracing, in the flinching, in the disappearing during sex, in the ways I managed and managed and managed what my husband experienced as rejection.

There is something else research tells us that almost nobody talks about. Many survivors do not abstain from sex after assault. Many become hypersexual, seeking encounter after encounter, not from desire but from the body’s attempt to reclaim control over the very thing that made it feel powerless. To prove: I am fine. This doesn’t affect me. The irony being that this response is itself the evidence of how much it does.

I know this from the inside. I lived versions of it. The body finding its own way through what the mind had sealed off.

I remembered Spain in a BDSM class, years later. While being flogged. The memory came through the body, as body memories always do, when the conditions are finally safe enough. Not as narrative. As sensation first, then fragment, then the whole shape of it.

I came to London. I recreated the scene with a Dom. I gave it a different ending. I was finally the one who chose.

Switzerland

Three years later, at university in Switzerland, there was another night.

A date. A drink. I woke up in a bed with two men, him and his flatmate. Bite marks across my body. Bruises everywhere. My arms were tied to the bed.

No memory of getting there. No memory of consenting to anything. No way to defend myself. No way to leave.

I was young. I was far from home. I had no language for what had happened and no framework that would have held me if I had tried to name it.

So I didn’t name it. The way you do when there is nothing to hold it.

I am not telling you this to be brave. I am telling you this because it is where everything I do comes from. And because when I read the CNN investigation, I was not reading it as a practitioner. I was reading it as someone who knows exactly what those women woke up to. The confusion. The body that knows before the mind has words. The years of carrying something with no container for it.

The perpetrators in those Telegram groups understood something that survivors understand from the inside: if she can’t remember, there is no evidence. They chose their drugs accordingly. What they did not account for is that the body keeps its own record. It always does. And that record surfaces, in the bracing, in the scanning, in the flinching when a hand reaches for her, even a hand she loves.

What CNN Found

CNN published their investigation in March 2026, the findings of months of undercover reporting. A pornographic website called Motherless.com received 62 million visits in February alone, its core audience in the United States. Among the content it hosts: more than 20,000 videos tagged as “sleep” content. Videos of women filmed while unconscious. The categories used are #passedout and #eyecheck.

Eyecheck. Men lifting the closed eyelids of sedated women on camera. To document that she is fully unconscious before they continue. Some of those videos have surpassed 50,000 views.

Linked from that site, CNN found a Telegram group called “Zzz.” Nearly a thousand members. They traded advice on which drugs to use, what doses, how to time it so the toxicology window closes before she wakes up and gets herself to a hospital. They shared videos of the assaults. They organised livestreams, $20 per viewer, cryptocurrency only, the audience directing what happened on screen. One man advertised sedation liquid shipped worldwide. 150 euros a bottle. His promise: she won’t feel anything and she won’t remember anything.

She won’t remember anything.

The perpetrators have been deliberately shifting away from the drugs historically associated with date rape, Rohypnol, GHB, specifically because newer substances like zolpidem, sold as Ambien, exit the body in seven to eight hours. By the time a woman registers that something may have happened, collects herself, and gets to a hospital, the evidence is already gone. This is not opportunistic. The WHO notes that reliable data on drug-facilitated sexual assault is scarce by design, it is among the most severely underreported categories of violence precisely because the mechanism of the crime removes the evidence and the memory simultaneously.

This is not random violence in a dark alley. This is organised, methodical, community-supported violation of women in their own homes, in their own beds, by the men who share those beds with them. A French lawmaker who was herself drugged by a former senator called these groups an online rape academy, where every subject is taught.

The investigation connects directly to the Pelicot case, Gisèle Pélicot, drugged and raped more than 200 times by 70 men while her husband Dominique organised the invitations through a chat group he named “Without Her Knowledge.” That site was shut down after the trial. The communities it served did not disappear. They migrated.

The Debate Was About the Numbers

When the story circulated on social media, something predictable happened.

Men started debating the numbers.

It wasn’t 62 million men at a rape academy. It was 62 million total visits to a site with many categories. Get your facts right.

@straight_line_and_bourbon, a man with 60,000 followers who is worth paying attention to, said it more clearly than I could:

If a woman says 62 million visits to a rape site in one month and your first move is “well, the numbers”, you already missed the entire point.

The story is men drugging, raping, and livestreaming their wives and partners for profit.

I want to stay with that deflection for a moment. Because I recognise it. The instinct to check the precision of the statistic before feeling the weight of what the statistic describes, that instinct is a pattern. It is the same pattern that allows communities to calcify quietly. That allows a thousand men to gather in a group and trade this kind of advice without anyone in their lives saying anything.

Is there a number that would make this acceptable? Is there a smaller count of men lifting a sedated woman’s eyelid on camera to confirm she is unconscious that would make you feel better?

I am asking genuinely.

To the Men Who Are Speaking Up

Many men are not doing this. Many men are disgusted and saying so publicly. @jedayal, @britlkoch, @lifeandalex, @thecaterussel, @presxrepeat are among the voices I have been reading this week, men naming this clearly, from their own platforms, without hedging. @sigourneybelle, who holds a lineage I trained in, is also speaking about it, and her voice here matters particularly to me.

What you are doing matters. Not because your voice is more important than the voices of the women who have been living this. But because the men in those Telegram groups did not appear from nowhere. They came from communities, friendships, families, and silences. The places where nothing was said.

You are what gets said in those spaces. Keep going.

Momma, Shouldn’t These Things Be Private?

My sons asked me this. Not once. More than once, over the years.

I understood the question. I am their mother. I wore the Notting Hill PTA version of myself for years, the yummy mummy on the school run, the one who kept the family together across three countries, the one who made everything look contained. I toned down the “orgasm expert” billing. I stopped leading with certain parts of my story. I understood that they needed me to be legible to the world in a way that felt safe for them.

What I could not do was stop knowing what I know.

I have two sons and two daughters. My daughters are in a world where a site called Motherless.com, a name that should give every one of us pause, received 62 million visits in one month. My sons are in a world where those Telegram members had friends, families, communities, and nobody said anything.

My sons understand now. They are proud of what I do. That shift, from Momma, shouldn’t these things be private? to pride, did not happen because I explained it to them. It happened because they watched what this work does. They watched women come back to themselves. They watched men stop second-guessing. They watched what happens when someone finally has the language for what their body has been holding.

The reason I can stay with what most practitioners never get near is precisely because I have lived what most practitioners only study. The Spain party at eighteen. The Switzerland bed at twenty-one. The years of carrying both with no container. The BDSM class where the memory finally surfaced, the only way it could. The Dom in London who helped me give it a different ending.

Every modality I hold, I found because I needed it first. I did not build a toolkit. I followed a trail. And because I followed it all the way, into the places that are not in the textbooks, into the experiences that require you to have actually been there, I can now work with clients in those same places without flinching.

That is not a credential. It is a life. And I have stopped being quiet about it.

What 27 Years Teaches You

I know the specific quality of the bracing, that millisecond of preparation when a hand reaches for you, even a hand you love, even a hand you want. I know the exhaustion of performing relaxation instead of actually being relaxed. I know what it costs, every single day, to manage from the outside what is happening on the inside.

The body is not failing when it does this. It never was.

The bracing, the scanning, the disappearing, these are the body’s intelligence doing exactly what intelligence does: protecting what is most vulnerable until the conditions are finally safe enough to let it move. What I do is not fix what is wrong. What I do is create the conditions where what has been held can finally move.

This month, a woman moved from a 9 to a 2 on something she had been carrying for years. She is herself a seasoned practitioner. She trains others in this work. She had been in therapy for years and had never felt safe enough to go there. Within forty minutes, without retelling the story, without going back into the moment, her body moved it.

She is not unusual. She is what I see every single week.

Why Now

This essay was supposed to come next month.

I am publishing it now because those Telegram group members are still somewhere. Because the woman who had Ambien crushed into her nightly cup of tea for years by her husband of sixteen years is still living in the aftermath. Because I woke up in a bed in Switzerland at twenty-one and spent years not having the language for it. Because I was eighteen in Spain and fought until I passed out.

I have the language now. I spend every working day helping women find theirs.

The work I offer lives right next to the question of how it can go so wrong.

Still following the breadcrumbs.
Juliette
