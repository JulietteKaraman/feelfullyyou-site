Asking First Is The Part That Brings Her Back

Most men think saying it out loud will end the evening. One question, one touch and her answer on a stoplight will tell you she is still in the room.

By Juliette Karaman · 22 November 2026

You’re on the sofa. Her feet are up, her shoulder is a few inches from yours, and you’re looking at the place where her neck meets her collarbone.

Your hand’s half decided already. You can feel it in your forearm, that small lift before the move.

Then the second thing arrives. A tightening in your chest. A swallow.

If you ask, the evening might end. If you don’t ask, she might go still in the way you’ve learned not to notice.

So the hand hovers. Two seconds. Three.

Dear men who love women, that hover is the most honest thing you do all day, and I want to show you what to do with it.

“We’ve been together for years. I’m not asking permission to touch my own partner.”

Fair. You love her, and you’ve Touched her more times than either of you could count.

Years together gave your hands the route. Her body still decides, every single time, whether to come along.

In a body that’s been Touched for years without a word attached, the first second goes like this. It reads the hand before the hand arrives. Is this hello? Is this a request? Is this the start of something I now have to manage?

That’s a lot of work for the first second of a Touch. A woman whose body is busy working it out is busy, and busy is not where she comes back from.

“Won’t asking kill the mood?”

That’s the fear that keeps your hand hovering.

So listen to how they do it on a film set.

Michael Douglas talked about it on Happy Sad Confused this October. Before a sex scene, the actors talk it through, slowly, before the camera rolls.

“Is it all right to touch your breasts?”

She says yes. Then it happens.

He called trust the big thing with those scenes. On that set, the question is how the scene gets made.

I’ll be straight with you about the science. I can’t find a study that tests whether asking out loud changes the mood, so I won’t pretend there is one.

What I can tell you is what I see with the men I work with. They’re afraid to ask “May I?”, afraid that saying anything at all will kill the mood. It doesn’t. Asking is consent, and it gets her to check in with herself: am I a hell yes to this, right now?

When something is hard for her to say, it goes underground.

So make the answer easy. She gets to skip the explaining and the searching for words.

She just gives you a colour.

I’ll tell you what it does in my own body.

When Alex asks “May I?”, I say “You may.” Out loud, every time. You’ll hear it in my videos.

Each time I say it, I’m teaching my body that this is a Touch I’ve said yes to. My body has been through rape. Whatever happened before gets rewired, one “You may” at a time, until a hand arriving means something good is coming. It may take years, and it’s necessary.

“Doesn’t she want me to just know?”

You were handed a story, and I call it The Spontaneity Lie. If the love is real, you’ll just know what she wants, and closeness will arrive on its own.

So you tried harder, and the guessing got louder, and her body read louder as one more thing to brace against.

I never ask a woman how she wants to be Touched. I teach the Touch Map, which finds out as it goes. One part of the body at a time, and she says green, orange or red while it is happening. Nobody has to know in advance. You find out, out loud, together.

You can borrow a small piece of that today.

The Fix: one round

Solo first. Before you ask her anything, do it on your own body. I always do the Touch Map as a self-Touch date first, alone, before any partner version, and there’s a reason. You need to know what it’s like to be asked, and what it’s like to answer.

Pick one place. Your forearm will do. Touch it one way, slowly. Say out loud, to the empty room, green, orange or red. Change the Touch. Say the colour again.

Watch what happens. Your body answers faster than your head does.

Then the round with her. Choose a moment when nothing is happening, and tell her what you would like to try. One round. Three colours. Green is yes, go for it. Orange is maybe, pause, change the Touch. Red is no Touch at all, stop.

She is allowed to say red to the whole idea. That’s a perfectly good answer, and you thank her for it.

If she is willing, pick one place and one Touch before you begin. Her shoulder, a flat hand. Then say it:

“Is it all right to touch you here?”

If those words feel stiff in your mouth, use your own. Keep it to one place and one Touch, and keep it a question she can answer with a colour. A sentence that needs a speech back from her has missed the point of this.

Wait. Touch only after you have heard a colour. The wait will feel long. It’s about four seconds.

  • Green: go ahead. Stay with the same Touch for as long as she stays green, because she can change the colour at any point.

  • Orange: pause. Make the Touch lighter, or slower, or move it somewhere else, then ask again about the new one.

  • Red: hand away, straight away. Say “Thank you.” No “why”, no sulk, no second attempt in a different voice.

That’s one round. One question, one Touch, one colour. Watch her while it happens. Her shoulder may drop first. Her breath may change, or she may laugh because it feels odd to be asked after all these years... Whatever comes, you’ve just given her body something it rarely gets, which is a say before the hand lands. Stop there if you like. You’ve done the most important thing already, because you have shown her body, in real time, that her answer changes what your hand does.

“What if she says red every time?”

Then you’ve learned something you couldn’t have learned by guessing, and you took it well, which is what keeps the door unlocked.

Three things to take

1. Do the round on your own body first. Forearm, one Touch, one colour out loud. You can’t ask well until you’ve answered.

2. Ask about one Touch you’ve already chosen, then wait. The colour comes before the hand lands.

3. Thank her for every colour, red included. The red you receive well is the reason there’ll be a next green.

Picture a year from now, unchanged. The same sofa, the same half-second hover, a hand arriving without a word, and her body working out again which kind of hand this is.

Or a sentence that takes four seconds, and a colour.
