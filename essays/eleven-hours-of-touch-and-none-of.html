Eleven Hours of Touch and None of It Was Mine

Being climbed on all day and being touched are two different things. That difference is why his hand at ten at night lands like one more thing being asked of you.

By Juliette Karaman · 4 November 2026

What the Body Knows

By nine in the morning I had been touched more than most people are touched in a week.

I would have paid actual money for nobody to come near me again until Thursday.

Someone had been in my bed since four.

Someone hung off my leg while I made the toast. A hand in my hair. A foot in my ribs. A body that needed carrying up the stairs while I held two bags.

A cup of tea still sitting on the hall table where I put it down. Cold. With a skin on it.

Then at ten that night a hand arrives on your hip, and something in you goes rigid before you have decided anything at all.

Rigid. Before thought.

“So you don’t like being touched.”

I love being touched. That is what made it so confusing.

A woman wrote to her husband asking him not to touch her for three to five years. She put a range on it.

She said her desire to be touched was gone by nine in the morning.

Then she added the line that gives the whole thing away:

“I promise it is not you, it is me.”

She is right. She is also apologising for something that does not need an apology, because nobody ever gave her the language for what is actually happening.

“Alright, so what IS happening?”

Touch has a direction, and your body counts the two directions separately.

There is touch you give. That is your day.

A child climbing you is a demand being met by you. Your body is the surface, the regulator, the safe place somebody else comes to.

Every one of those takes something out of you. Attention. Patience. A steady arm. A face that stays pleasant.

(The face is the tiring one. Nobody warns you about the face.)

Then there is touch you receive. Where nothing is asked of you. Where you do not have to be steady for anyone, or careful how you respond.

You have had eleven hours of the first and roughly none of the second.

Your body is full in one direction and starving in the other, and both of those are true at once.

I have a name for it, from years of watching it in the room. Overtouched, underfelt.

Stimulation without surrender.

I see it in mothers constantly, women who would laugh in your face if you called them touch-deprived, because their hands have been full since dawn.

“So what’s my body actually doing at ten at night?”

Maths. In under half a second.

It is asking something else entirely, and it has nothing to do with whether you love him. “Is this another one that is going to require something from me?”

If the answer is yes, or even might be, it closes the shutters. There is nothing left in the give column and no evidence yet that this one is coming from the other one.

That flinch is an accounting error. It is not a verdict on him, and neither of you has the words for it yet.

I have four children. I know the exhaustion of a body that has been useful all day, and I know exactly what it did to me, because I overrode every bit of it and kept going.

There was a version of me who could stand at the sink at nine at night with my jaw tight and my breath held, and still say “I’m fine” if anyone asked, because anything else felt like letting the whole house down.

A brace nobody can see is a brace nobody can repair.

“Can’t I just push through it?”

That is one of the two things you have already tried.

More touch. Push through, make yourself available, do it for the relationship. That adds to the column that is already overflowing, and your body braces harder.

Less touch. Hold him off until you feel like it. You wait, he waits, the waiting becomes the weather in the house, and the feeling never arrives on its own, because nothing has changed about the accounting.

How long have you been choosing between those two?

The Fix

A very small amount of touch that is entirely yours, taken before anybody asks you for anything.

The Threshold. Before your hand touches the front door, stop. Palm flat on your own chest. Three slow breaths, the out-breath longer than the in. Then go in.

Ten seconds. On the doorstep, in the car park, outside the school gates.

The Full Stop. When you hit your limit, notice out loud, then stop. “I am at my limit for touch right now.”

No question mark. No apology. The question mark is where you hand it back for approval.

Three things to take

1. Count the directions separately. Eleven hours of give and none of receive is not the same as a full day of touch, whatever it looks like on paper.

2. Take your ten seconds before the door, not after. After, you are already the safe place for everyone in there.

3. Say the limit without the question mark. It will feel unbearably rude the first time. Within a fortnight the house will be using it back at you, and that is when you find out everybody had a limit and nobody had a way to say it.

What comes back first is not desire. Everyone waits for desire and then despairs when it does not turn up.

What comes back first is noticing. Somewhere around the second week you catch yourself registering that a hand is on your shoulder, rather than simply enduring it.

Nobody else in the room will see that happen.

You have been the safe place for everyone in that house since four o’clock this morning.

Nobody has been the safe place for you.

You have got so good at it that you stopped noticing it was missing.
