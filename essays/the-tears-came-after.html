The Tears Came After

Why do I cry after sex when it was good? Nearly half the women in one study had felt sad, tearful or irritable after sex they wanted. The one study that looked found no link to how close she was to hi

By Juliette Karaman · 15 November 2026

Your cheek is on his chest. His breathing slows down under your ear. It was good. Your legs are still humming with it.

Then your throat closes.

There’s a half-second just before it closes. Remember that half-second. We’ll come back to it.

Your eyes sting, and before you can catch it there’s a wet patch spreading across his skin……

He goes very still. “Did I hurt you?”

You say no. You say “I don’t know.” You say “it’s nothing, honestly, I’m fine,” and you wipe your face with the back of your hand and hope he doesn’t ask again.

Because you genuinely don’t know. Nothing went wrong.

That’s the part that frightens you.

“Why am I crying when it was good?”

It has a name. Researchers call it postcoital dysphoria: tears, sadness or irritability after sex that was wanted, consensual, and by every measure you’ve got, good.

In 2015 a research team asked 230 women at university about it. Forty-six per cent had felt it at least once in their lives. About five per cent had felt it in the month before.

That’s a small study, students, an online survey. I’m telling you that because you deserve the honest size of it. Even so, nearly one in two.

That is a LOT of women wiping their faces on the duvet and saying “I’m fine.”

The same research group went back a few years later and asked men. 1,208 of them. Forty-one per cent said yes, at some point. One in five said it had happened in the previous four weeks.

So the man whose chest you just cried on may know this feeling from the inside too….

We’re sold the afterglow as something that just arrives, on its own, every time. When it doesn’t arrive, we read the tears as a verdict on him, on us, on the whole relationship. Those five minutes after sex have no structure in them at all. So let’s give them some.

“So it means I don’t love him?”

The 2015 study looked for a link between these tears and closeness in the relationship. They didn’t find one. Women in close, loving relationships cried. Women in distant ones cried.

What I see over and over in the women I work with is a body that holds all week. Holding the house, the diary, the face she wears at work, the face she wears for everyone. Then for as long as it lasts, something gets through and she stops holding……

What the holding was keeping back gets a way out. For a lot of women, that way out is tears.

That’s a body letting go of a load it was carrying in the background. It needs space.

“Then why does it feel so awful?”

Because sometimes it IS more than release. I’m not going to pretend otherwise.

The two studies asked different questions, and I think the gap between them is the most useful thing in them. The women’s study tested closeness, and found no link. The men’s study tested other things, and found these tears were associated with current psychological distress, with some sexual difficulties, and with childhood sexual abuse.

An association across a group says nothing certain about one person. Please don’t diagnose yourself from a survey of men.

It does mean both can be true.

For a lot of people, the tears are the body exhaling.

For some, sex is the one moment the guard drops far enough for something older to surface. My own body kept something for twenty-five years before my mind would look at it. I know how that works from the inside.

A bigger study in 2021, more than four thousand people, built a proper measure of the minutes after sex. In a woman’s answers it found four separate threads. Feeling close to the person beside her was one. Feeling emotionally overwhelmed was another. They can arrive in the same five minutes, in the same body, and neither one cancels the other.

Your body already knows which kind of tears these are. The work is learning to ask it.

“When is it more than that?”

Read this part before you try anything below.

If the tears come with fear, with pictures you didn’t ask for, with a pull to leave your own body or to get away from his, that’s your body asking for proper support. That is worth acting on.

If you said yes and your body was saying something else, that deserves a person, not an article.

A trauma-trained practitioner can help you go there safely, at your pace. EMDR is one of the things I’m trained in.

If any of this touches sexual violence, in your past or now, Rape Crisis runs a free 24/7 support line for anyone aged 16 and over in England and Wales: 0808 500 2222. If you’re in immediate danger, call 999.

If any of that starts happening while you do the practice below, stop.

“What do I actually do when it happens?”

There’s a practice I teach called The Threshold. Palm flat on your own chest, three breaths. I usually give it for the moment before you walk through your own front door. It belongs here too, in that half-second before your throat closes.

Slowly, then. One at a time.

Stay where you are if you want to stay. If his chest feels like too much, roll onto your back. Both are fine.

Bring your own palm to the middle of your chest and leave it there. Flat. Your hand, your choice.

Take three breaths, slowly.

On each one, notice the body sensation under your hand. You’re looking for the FELT sense, the sensation itself, before the story about it arrives.

Then give him one sentence, so he isn’t left guessing in the dark: “I don’t know what this is yet. Stay with me.”

If you’re the man lying there with a wet chest and your partner crying on it…. the hardest thing and the best thing are the same thing. Don’t try to fix it. Ask one question, “Closer, or room?”, and do whichever comes back.

The lesson

  1. Name it. Postcoital dysphoria. A named thing can be mentioned over toast the next morning.

  2. Put your hand on it. Palm flat, three breaths, find the sensation before the story.

  3. Let him stay. One sentence, so neither of you spends the next hour guessing.

Picture it a year from now, unchanged. Same throat. Same back of the hand. Same “I’m fine.”

Or the tears come, your palm stays flat on your chest, and he stays.
