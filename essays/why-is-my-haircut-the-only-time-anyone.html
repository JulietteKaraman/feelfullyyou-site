Why Is My Haircut the Only Time Anyone Touches Me?

Skin hunger is a real state with a real cost. The way back starts with your own hands, tonight, and it does not need anyone else to turn up.

By Juliette Karaman · 12 August 2026

You booked the haircut four days early, and you know exactly why.

It's the wash. The warm water, the hard rim of the basin under the back of your neck, the smell of a shampoo you would never buy for yourself, someone's fingers moving slowly across your scalp with no agenda in them at all.

You keep your eyes closed longer than you need to. You tip more than you can afford.

On the walk home there's a feeling you've never found a word for. Half fed. Half ashamed of how much that meant.

So you type the question you wouldn't say out loud: why is my haircut the only time anyone touches me?

Part One: Your body keeps a count you never agreed to

There's a word for this that people find on their own, out in the wild, long before anyone teaches it to them. Skin hunger.

I read a thread recently where a man had written down the day of his monthly haircut like an appointment with someone he loved. Underneath it, a hundred other people said the same thing in their own words......

The massage they book when nothing hurts. The manicure for nails they don't care about. One woman wrote that she'd gone years without contact, and asked, only half joking, whether there was a club she could join where people simply hugged.

Here's what I'm actually seeing in my clients, over and over, in this exact shape: a grown adult, functioning, employed, loved even, who cannot remember the last time a hand landed on them for no reason. My DMs are full of it. Not the dramatic version. The quiet one, the "I'm probably being silly but..." one, sent at midnight by someone who has never once said this out loud to a person's face.

A body reporting an actual deficit. That's what it is. Not neediness, whatever the voice in your head calls it at 11pm.

Your skin isn't decoration, by the way…… it carries a set of nerve fibres, C-tactile afferents, that fire specifically for slow, warm, gentle contact at roughly the speed of a stroke down a forearm. About the pace of the hairdresser's hands, as it happens…

They don't report pressure or temperature to the thinking part of you. They report this is safe, you are not alone, straight into the parts of the brain that handle emotion and belonging.

When that traffic goes quiet for long enough, the body doesn't simply carry on unbothered. Sleep gets worse.

The baseline hum of alertness climbs. Small slights land harder than they should. You become the one who gets called "tired," or "prickly," or "a bit much lately," and every one of those words lands on your character when the thing that has actually happened is that no hand has been on you in weeks.

The count is being kept whether you agreed to it or not. The hairdresser isn't a substitute for love, she's just the only entry in the ledger this month.

When was the last hand on you that wanted nothing back? If you have to work it out on your fingers, that's the count....

Part Two: Your own hands are not the consolation prize

Here's where nearly everyone goes wrong, and I understand why, because it's the story everyone hands you first. You decide the answer is a person.

A partner, a new relationship, a friend who hugs properly. So you wait. How long have you been waiting for that person to arrive, and what has it cost you already?

You wait for the right body to turn up and give you back what you're missing, and the waiting itself becomes another month of nothing....

The body doesn't know the difference between hands the way your story about it does…… it knows slow, it knows warm, it knows predicted. Your own hands qualify.

I learned this on the least romantic ground imaginable. My first career was with autistic children, teaching them to feel safe in their own skin.

I'd watch a small body go rigid the second a hand moved toward it from behind, out of sight, without warning. Then I'd watch that same body stay soft, even lean in, when the same hand approached slowly, in view, at a pace it could actually track. Same hand. Same child. The only thing that changed was whether the nervous system saw it coming.

That is where I first understood the rule I still teach: a predicted touch is a safe touch.

Your nervous system is not asking who. It's asking whether it can see it coming.

There were years, later, when I couldn't feel my own body at all. I gave and gave and couldn't take anything in, and my body eventually stopped asking politely about it.

What brought me back wasn't being touched by the right person, it was learning that sensation is a language, and starting to speak it with my own two hands....

So here are two practices, whole, free, tonight. They're from my own bank and they're both designed for one person alone in a room.

Try this tonight. Hand on your own heart.

One hand flat on your chest, one hand on your belly. One minute, no more.

You are not trying to relax. You are noticing two things: the warmth of your own hands coming back at you through your own skin, and the breath moving underneath them. Feel where the warmth arrives first. Feel the rise under the lower hand.

One minute. Then stop.

My line about this has stayed the same for years, because it keeps proving itself. A body that cannot receive its own touch will struggle to receive anyone else's.

You are not practising for solitude. You are re-opening the channel that anyone else would eventually have to come through.

Then this. Touch Base®.

Bring the pad of your thumb to the side of your forefinger. Move it slower than feels natural.

Slower than you think slow is. Like honey.

Feel every ridge of your own fingerprint as you go. The urge to speed up will arrive within about fifteen seconds.

Notice it. Let it pass. Keep going at honey pace.

While you do it, one true sentence, silently or out loud: there is no tiger in the room. I am safe right now.

Two minutes, morning and evening. Before you start, rate how settled your body feels from one to ten.

Rate it again after. Write both numbers somewhere you will see them.

The numbers are the POINT. Your mind will tell you nothing is happening.

A fortnight of before-and-after numbers in your own handwriting says otherwise. At around thirty-one days it starts running on its own, and at ninety it lives in your hands, available in a supermarket queue, the moment of road rage when someone cuts in front of you, in a meeting, in the ten seconds before you walk through your own front door.

Part Three: What changes when the count starts going up

You'll feel two things quite quickly, and neither is what people expect……

The first is that the hunger gets louder before it gets quieter. A body that's been running on almost nothing tends to go quiet about it, and then you give it a minute of real contact and it remembers, all at once, what it's been going without.

Some people cry the first week doing something this small. I've had grown men tell me this sheepishly, like it's a confession.

If that's you, nothing has gone wrong. That's a channel opening after a long time closed.

The second is that you become easier to reach. I watch this one happen over and over (it's never the bit anyone comes to me for)....

A person who's fed their own skin for a few weeks stops arriving at every interaction slightly starving. The hug from a friend lands instead of bouncing off.

The hand on the back from a partner gets received rather than filed. You stop needing every single contact to make up a whole month's shortfall.

If you do have someone in the house, this is where they come back into it. Skip the conversation about how you never touch any more.

Make a small offer that costs nothing to receive: your palm flat between their shoulder blades as you pass behind them, resting there rather than patting, gone before they have to respond to it. Then again tomorrow.

The ledger fills the way it emptied. In tiny entries nobody would think to notice.

Your body braced. Patterns can be changed.

That woman in the hairdresser's chair, eyes closed, tipping too much…… she's accurate. Her body knows exactly what it needs and has found the one place in her week where it's legally available.

I see this constantly, by the way, in women who would never call themselves touch-starved, would be almost offended by the phrase. All I'm doing is showing her she doesn't have to wait four weeks and pay for it.

Still following the breadcrumbs.
Juliette
