Freeze Looks Like Permission

Freeze is the third gear, after fight and after flight, and it arrives looking like permission. This is what her body runs when nothing appears to be happening.

By Juliette Karaman · 8 November 2026

What the Body Knows

He waited until she had gone up to bed before he could bring himself to type it.

Eleven ten on a Tuesday night. Television on mute, because he did not want her coming back down to ask what he was doing.

She has never once said no to him.

That is the part he cannot say out loud to anyone.

He got it out in one sentence and then apologised for the sentence.

“She doesn’t say no. She just lays there.”

Then, underneath it:

“I feel like a monster for even noticing.”

Stay with that second line, because it is the reason this goes unspoken for years at a time.

He is not describing a refusal. A refusal he could work with. A refusal gives you something to answer.

He is describing a yes with nobody home behind it.

He has no complaint he is allowed to make. She agreed. She did not push him away. She was there.

She was there, and she was not there, and both of those are true at once.

“But she says yes.”

She does. That is exactly the trouble.

You were handed two settings for a body under threat. Fight. Flight.

She snaps at you, or she leaves the room. Both are loud. Both at least give you something to answer.

There is a third gear underneath those two, and it engages when the body has worked out that neither fighting nor leaving is available.

Stephen Porges called it the dorsal state. Oldest branch of the system, and the slowest.

When it comes on, the body powers down. Heart rate can drop. Breath goes high and thin. Muscle tone goes slack. Sensation thins out, which is the body’s own anaesthetic, and it is remarkably good at its job.

Freeze is the most complete response a body has.

Animals do this. Documented for over a century, in every mammal anyone has studied for it. The mouse in the cat’s mouth goes limp.

Nobody watching would call that mouse relaxed.

In that state she can still speak. She can still stroke your arm. She can still say yes.

The parts of her that run the social performance stay online long after the parts that feel anything have gone quiet.

So you get consent. You get company. You get a hand on your back, and nothing behind any of it, and not one moment you can point at and say there, that is where it went wrong.

“How would you know?”

Because I was her.

I was in a long relationship with a man I loved. After the children came I could not bear to be touched.

When we did have sex I wanted toys, I wanted a lot of stimulation, I wanted volume, because volume was the only thing loud enough to reach me through the anaesthetic.

Afterwards I would cry and have no idea why.

I thought I had gone cold. I thought something in me had closed for good.

What had actually happened is that my body was still running a protocol it installed decades earlier, and I had no access to the file.

The memory that explained it surfaced years later. In a room, in my body, while I was doing something entirely unrelated to therapy. It came up through the tissue.

So when a man describes his partner going still under his hand, I am not reading that off a page.

“She’s just tired.”

A tired body still leans in. A frozen body lies very still and lets things happen to it.

That reading is kind, it is often partly true, and it is the reason nothing changes for eleven years.

There are two others, and they cost as much.

“She’s not attracted to me anymore.” Fastest and most expensive. It turns her nervous system state into a verdict on your face, your body, your worth. Once you carry that verdict you reach less. She registers the drop as proof the distance is mutual. Neither of you says a word.

“I’ll make it better. More romance, more attention.” Good intention. The effect is that the pressure on her body goes UP. She now has to be present, and appreciative, and responsive to a candlelit weekend you paid for.

Every increase in warmth is also an increase in demand, unless you take the destination off the table first.

Five things to read instead

You cannot see her nervous system. You can see what it does to the outside of her, and once you know these you will not miss them again.

Her breath. Where does it live. Freeze breathing sits high in the chest, small and fast, or it holds without her noticing. A body with something available in it breathes into the belly, and the ribs move sideways.

Her stillness. There is settled stillness and there is held stillness. Settled is heavy. It sinks towards you. Held is light, poised, faintly suspended, like something waiting for the thing to be over.

Her eyes. In freeze the gaze fixes on one point, or unfocuses somewhere past your shoulder. She is looking at you and there is no traffic in the looking.

Her hands. Cold. Blood is being pulled to the middle. This one is measurable and she has no say in it.

The follow. Rest your hand on her forearm and stay there. A body with something available in it makes a tiny movement towards your hand within a few seconds. A millimetre of lean. A small settle.

A frozen body waits.

That millimetre tells me more in five seconds than an hour of talking.

“Twenty-three years though?”

Twenty-three.

A couple came to me who had not had sex since the eighties. They still loved each other. They had never once had a fight about it, which tells you how carefully it had been managed on both sides.

He had spent two decades reading her stillness as a decision she had made about him.

She had spent two decades unable to explain that she was choosing nothing at all.

They took a second honeymoon three months later.

He told me the thing that turned it was the day he stopped trying to reach her and started reading her.

The Fix

Three minutes. A timer she can see.

Three. Not twenty. Twenty would blow her system wide open, and even three can be long.

Say out loud before you start that nothing else happens after it.

Timer goes, you stop. Even if it is going well.

Build to fifteen minutes over a few weeks. Never in one evening.

Three things to take

1. Read her, do not reach for her. The millimetre of lean in the first five seconds will tell you more than the conversation you have been rehearsing.

2. Take the destination off the table out loud, before you touch her. Not implied. Said.

3. Stop while it is going well. Nothing you do will make touch safe faster than being the man who stopped when he did not have to.

She is still in there.

She went somewhere her body could survive the years she was in, and it has been holding the door for her ever since, waiting for a reason to believe it can let go of the handle.

You are allowed to be that reason.

You are also allowed to have found this unbearably lonely, all this time, while doing nothing wrong.
