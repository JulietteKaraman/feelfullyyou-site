Why Do I Feel Disgusted When He Touches Me?

Disgust is the biggest lever your body has, and it only reaches for it after the smaller signals have stopped working. Here is what it is protecting, and the way back.

By Juliette Karaman · 2 September 2026

You are standing at the sink and his hand arrives on your lower back, and something in you pulls away so fast you have to hold still to cover it.

He means it kindly. That is the part that undoes you.

Then the second wave lands, about a second and a half behind the first, and it is the worse of the two. The recoil you could almost live with. It is the shame arriving on top of it that keeps you awake.

Because you love him. Nothing has happened. He has done nothing except put a warm hand on his partner's back at half past nine on a Tuesday.

So at one in the morning, phone tilted away from him, you type the sentence you would never say out loud: why do I feel disgusted when he touches me?

Part One: A flinch and this are two different animals

A flinch is fast. Something moves at the edge of your vision, your shoulder jumps, and four seconds later you are fine and slightly embarrassed. Startle comes and goes.

What you are describing does not go. It arrives with the touch, it spreads, it has a taste to it, and it leaves a residue that lasts the rest of the evening.

It also carries something a flinch never does. A moral flavour. A sense that the wrongness is somehow yours.

Disgust was built as a rejection response, the body's way of saying this does not come in. Fear makes you run. Disgust makes you expel.

It began with food and rot and contamination, then got recruited for anything the body decided must be kept out. Paul Rozin spent a career mapping how far it travels once it is off the leash. It stops being about spoiled meat quite quickly, and attaches to people, to touch, to whole categories of contact.

Disgust is the last move your body makes. It has already tried everything quieter.

Your body says no in an order, and the order is quiet at the start. First there is a stillness that nobody sees, including you. A very small withholding, a fraction of breath held, a two-degree drop in something you have no name for.

If that gets read, it never escalates. Most of the time it does not get read, often because you were the one who trained yourself not to send it clearly.

So it goes up a rung. You turn slightly. You reach for the tea towel. You start a sentence about the bins.

When that gets missed too, month after month, year after year, the body stops spending energy on signals that never worked. It goes straight to the biggest lever it has left.

It picks the one response you cannot talk yourself out of. That is the whole design. You can override a subtle no. You can push through a turned shoulder. Nobody has ever reasoned themselves out of revulsion.

Your body is using the only language that has ever produced a result.

Part Two: The next forty minutes

So ask the question you have not been asking at one in the morning.

When his hand lands on your back at half nine, what is your body actually responding to?

The hand weighs about half a kilo. It is warm. On its own it is almost nothing.

Your body is responding to the next forty minutes it has learned to expect from that hand.

This is what runs underneath most of what I see. When touch has meant one thing for long enough, the body stops processing contact and starts processing the sequence. The hand becomes an opening move in a transaction whose ending is already known.

So it braces at the whole arc, arriving at once, compressed into the second the palm lands.

He puts a hand on you meaning I love you, and your nervous system receives here we go again, and both of you are being completely truthful about what just happened.

Stephen Porges gave this appraisal a name, neuroception, the body's continuous reading of safety and threat running well below anything you consciously decide. Your body finished its read while his hand was still in the air.

Everything you have ever been sold is aimed at making you want it more. Lingerie. Weekends away. Trying harder.

I have almost never seen wanting be the thing that was stuck.

A body with its foot flat on the brake needs to find out what it is braking for.

There is one pattern I see this run through more than any other. I call it the Armour. It contains in order to stay safe, and its particular signature is that softness feels dangerous where pressure does not.

Women in the Armour can often manage brisk, functional, sexual touch far better than they can manage tenderness. A hand held gently for too long is unbearable in a way that nothing rough ever is.

One client put it more clearly than I ever have. “Day 1 you had me bawling… softness and slow touch is triggering… I started crying immediately.”

If that sentence made something in you go very quiet, you already know which pattern you are running.

Part Three: What actually moves it

Three things do not work, and you have probably tried all three.

Pushing through. You let it happen and you go somewhere else while it does. Every time you do that you teach your body that its biggest signal changes nothing, and next time it must come in louder or earlier. Performing costs you capacity you are going to need later.

Explaining it to him in the moment. His hand is still on you, you are flooded, and now you are trying to construct a sentence that does not detonate. It will come out as a verdict on him. He will hear one.

Waiting for it to pass. This is the one I would happily go to war over. You have been sold the idea that desire is spontaneous, that it will simply return if you are patient and the conditions are right. It does not come back on its own, because nothing has changed about what your body is predicting. Every month of waiting is another month of rehearsing the same forecast.

What does move it is smaller and far less romantic than any of that.

You take the destination off the table, out loud, for a fixed period.

Six weeks, and both of you know it in advance. Nothing is allowed to lead anywhere, and that is the entire instruction.

Once the body stops predicting the arc, a hand goes back to being a hand. It can be assessed on its own terms instead of on years of forecast.

The furthest I have seen this go was a woman who had been sexually assaulted and had no memory of it. Her partner did not know how to approach her, or touch her, or be near her, without her body closing.

He kept reaching. She kept going somewhere else. He was certain he was doing it wrong.

He was doing everything right. Her body simply did not know that yet.

We spent a day together. I showed him how to hold still. How to approach. How to ground himself so that she could feel his stillness from where she was, and decide, on her own timing, whether to move towards it.

He stopped arriving with a destination attached, and her body had something it could read.

Ropes, as containment. A raspberry on her tongue. A silk blindfold. Her partner whispering good girl.

She felt herself again.

Here is what to expect if you start.

It gets louder before it gets quieter. A body that has been overridden for years will, given a genuine opening, tell you the size of the backlog all at once. The client I quoted earlier was crying on day one, over exactly this. Nothing has gone wrong when that happens. That is a channel opening after a long time shut.

Then the disgust starts arriving with information attached. It stops being one solid wall and gets specific. That hand, at that hour, with that thing behind it. Specific is workable. Specific can be asked about, and asked for differently.

Neutrality is the last thing to come back, and it is the cherry on top. A hand lands on you and it is just a hand. Nothing more. Nothing less.

For months you have read the recoil at the sink as evidence about the state of your love for him. It has been asking for something for a very long time, in the only language it had left, and nothing has answered it yet.

Your body braced. Patterns can be changed.

Still following the breadcrumbs.
