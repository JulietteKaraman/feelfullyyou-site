He Gets Defensive Every Time You Give Him Feedback

You said one true sentence, kindly. Four seconds later you're somewhere else entirely, and somehow you're the one apologising. Here is what actually happens in that gap, and the structure that closes it.

Juliette KaramanTouch and Intimacy Expert
A solo teaching from The Scrumptious Woman podcast

You say one sentence. "I felt a bit alone this week." One. True. Kind. Four, maybe five seconds later, you're somewhere else entirely. He's explaining how hard he works. Or reminding you of the time you did the same thing back in March. Or he's gone quiet and flat, offline completely, and you're the one saying, "no, no, no, I didn't mean it like that." You said one true thing in a kind voice, and somehow you're the one apologising.

I teach The Touch Reset: finding the patterns that close the door to touch, aliveness and connection, and learning how to open it again. I want to take you inside those four seconds, because the whole episode lives there.

What His Body Actually Hears

Rewind to the sentence. "I felt a bit alone." What his system received wasn't information. It received: you have failed her. Not a thought he chose. It arrived before your sentence had even finished, the way a flinch arrives before you've clocked it, the way your hand comes up before you've decided to catch the ball.

For a lot of men, being told they haven't provided something isn't feedback. It's a verdict on whether they're a good man at all. Feedback and indictment land in the exact same place in the body. And when the body reads incoming information as a verdict, it only has two moves. It argues back, or it flatlines. That's the defensiveness. That's the stonewall. Same brace, opposite sides of the same coin.

He's not defending against you. He's defending against the feeling of having failed you, one of the most unbearable feelings a man who loves you can carry, and it lands before he's even had a chance to think. I know this from the inside, not only from watching couples. I've done exactly the same thing myself.

What the Loop Actually Costs

So you learn. You stop saying things, or you round the edges off. You wait for a better moment that may never come. You decide it's not worth the forty minutes of repair afterward, and you say "I'm fine" instead. That's where intimacy goes to die, because he doesn't hear "I'm fine." He hears your temperature drop. He can't name what changed, he just knows the room got colder, and he braces without knowing why. You say even less next time. Neither of you built this loop on purpose. You've just been running it, probably for years.

Another date night won't touch this. You've already tried better wording, workshopped the same sentence in the shower, said it out loud on the drive home. It was never about the words being wrong. It's that there's no container around them, no edges, nowhere established for the conversation to go, and without that his body treats even a gentle sentence as an ambush.

Say the Shape of the Loop Out Loud

Do this now. You don't need him here. It's actually better if he isn't. Say the shape of your loop out loud, no blame in it, no names, just the shape. Mine goes: "You reach, I brace. You pull back, I feel alone." Say yours, whatever your actual steps are, three or four words each, no adjectives, no one at fault.

Notice what happens in your chest as you say it. For most people something drops, about half an inch. For the first time the shape is sitting outside you instead of inside you, something you've been carrying around as evidence about the relationship. That's the point. You cannot change a thing while you're still inside it. Once you can see it as a shape, you can see it as something happening between the two of you, not something wrong with either of you.

"It was never about him not caring. One of the bodies braced, and patterns can be changed."

The Dyad: Fifteen Minutes, One Prompt, Three Words

This is why I teach structure. It's called the Dyad, and I teach it exactly the way it was taught to me, the way I've used it on myself in my most painful times, including when I was trying to write my sister's eulogy and couldn't get the words out.

Fifteen minutes. Timer on. One prompt: "Tell me what you want me to know. I want to understand." One person speaks, the other listens, and the listener is only allowed three responses. "Thank you," if they've understood. "Clarify that," if they haven't. "Summarise that," to shorten it down to one thought. No defending, no context, no interjecting, no "but that's not what happened." He doesn't get to explain himself. Neither do you. That's what makes it survivable for both of you.

Most men hear "I want to talk" and brace for it to last forever. Fifteen minutes, both nervous systems can actually settle into, knowing there's an end. One person goes, says thank you, then you swap, same prompt, same rules. It feels stiff for the first few rounds. Then it goes deeper than either of you expected. I call it clearing the pipes. Couples who try this for the first time usually say "this isn't going to make a difference," and fifteen minutes later they're stunned by how much was actually in there.
