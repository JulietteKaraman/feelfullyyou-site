01

What is actually happening

You want to want it.

That is the part that is hard to say out loud, because it can sound like a complaint about your partner, and it isn't.

You love them. You're not bored. You may genuinely want closeness, intimacy and touch.

And still, sometimes your body answers before you've decided anything.

They reach for you and there's a tightening somewhere. A held breath. A small readiness. Maybe your attention leaves the sensation altogether.

Then comes the thinking.

Why did I do that?

Why can't I just enjoy this?

They're only trying to be affectionate.

What's wrong with me?

One of my clients described it like this:

"Why do I tense up every time he touches me? It's like my body goes stiff, and I really want to appreciate and enjoy his touch, but I don't know why I'm stiffening up."One of my clients

She is not doing it on purpose. Neither are you.

This is the part I want you to begin noticing.

  • A breath catching or shortening.
  • Shoulders creeping towards the ears.
  • A jaw setting.
  • Attention leaving the body and going up into the head.
  • Or going still and feeling almost nothing at all.

Not because any of those responses mean something is wrong.

Not because you need to make them go away.

None of this necessarily means you don't want your partner, or that you don't want touch.

Trying harder to enjoy it isn't necessarily the answer.

The first step is simply being able to notice:

  1. What actually happens to me when I'm being touched?
  2. What happens in my body?
  3. What can I actually feel?
  4. Where does my attention go?
  5. What changes when there is no expectation of where the touch needs to lead?

That's what we're going to find out.

02

Why date night does not fix it

A date night can have a destination.

Everyone at the table may know where the evening is supposed to end, which means every touch along the way can start to feel like a small step towards something.

His hand on your back at the restaurant is no longer just his hand on your back.

It is installment one.

When part of you is already anticipating where touch might lead, you're not only experiencing the touch that's happening now.

Part of you is already in what comes next.

So what happens if we take the destination away?

What happens when touch isn't the beginning of something else?

That is what The Trace does.

Both of you, first

Take a breath in through your nose, and exhale through your mouth. Again. In through the nose, out through the mouth.

If you are giving, four questions

  1. Can you follow the guidance rather than rushing ahead?
  2. Can you slow down when you may want to speed up?
  3. Can you actually follow the pace of my voice, without rushing ahead or trying to change what's happening?
  4. Can you touch without trying to get somewhere, or create a particular reaction?

If you are receiving, four questions

  1. Can you breathe, and can you soften enough to fully receive?
  2. Can you, for the next two minutes, stop managing how you are being touched?
  3. Can you allow your partner's touch to actually fully land, without the need to reciprocate?
  4. Without anticipating what is happening next?

The touch itself

Approach as the giver. Notice their jaw, their neck. Really look at it. Approach softly with your hand. You may ask them to close their eyes.

First, off the skin. Slowly, about one centimetre off the skin, start tracing with your hand from the earlobe, down the jaw, to the chin. Slightly off the skin the whole way.

Go back to the earlobe. This time let your fingers land, very softly.

Then the nail. If you can, with the nail, just stroke down to the chin.

Then the throat. Tilt their chin up, and bring those nails down the throat, lightly. Stop at the clavicle.

While you are receiving

Continue to receive.

Notice where you are actually looking at what they are doing, more than what you are receiving.

There is no right. There is no wrong in this. This is really what it is about. Learning to receive that sensation.

Which one are you today?
Showing both practices. Tap yours to see only what's yours.
If you are giving

Your job is not to impress anyone. It is to follow.

You may notice yourself wanting to get ahead of me. You may think, yes, yes, Juliette, I have got it, can we speed this up. You may get properly annoyed at how slowly I am asking you to move. Good. Notice that.

  • Can you follow anyway?
  • Can you slow down when I ask you to?
  • Can you resist turning a small movement into a bigger one?
  • Can you give touch without needing to get a reaction back?

Let simple be enough.

If you are receiving

You have an entirely different practice.

  • Can you actually receive?
  • Can you slow your breath?
  • Can you let the touch land rather than tracking where the hand is going next?
  • Can you notice the impulse to manage the person touching you?

To move towards their hand. To show them you like it. To give something back. To wonder what you are supposed to do.

For two minutes you do not have to do anything back.

Sink into the sensation that is actually here rather than reaching for the one you think should be here. Nothing needs manufacturing.

A laugh might come. You might soften. You might feel very close to him. You might feel awkward. You might feel almost nothing at all.

Whatever arrives is information.

When it finishes

Say nothing.

This is the part most couples get wrong, and it undoes the whole thing in one sentence.

  • Did you like that?
  • Was I doing it properly?
  • What did you feel?

Every one of those is kind, and every one of those hands the other person a job. Now they are not feeling what happened. They are managing how you feel about what happened.

Let it be quiet. Do not fill it.

The silence is the end of the practice, not an awkward gap before the real ending.

05

What you might notice

The first time you do this, you might want to laugh, speak, speed it up or get ahead of the guidance.

  • You might soften.
  • You might notice your breath change.
  • You might become very aware of the slowness.
  • You might notice yourself anticipating where the hand is going next.
  • You might want to help, perform, reciprocate or make something happen.
  • Or you might feel very little.

None of those responses is the goal, and none of them means you've done it wrong.

The practice is noticing what happens when touch is allowed to be this simple, and when neither of you has to take it anywhere.

If you do The Trace again, don't try to recreate what happened the first time.

Come back curious.
What can I feel today?

If you're wondering, "Did we do it right?"

You laughed the whole way through.+

Fine. You laughed. Notice it. You don't need to decide what the laughter means. Try The Trace again another time and see what happens then.

You felt almost nothing.+

Also information. Don't force yourself to feel more and don't make "nothing" mean something is wrong. You can come back to The Trace another day and notice whether the experience is the same or different.

The giver sped up.+

If you do it again, ask for the pace you want. One clear sentence is enough: "I wanted it slower."

Something came up that felt bigger than the exercise.+

Stop. Don't push through. This is a touch practice, not a therapy session. Stopping is allowed.

06

When you're ready to talk about it

Not immediately.

Let The Trace be complete first.

Let the silence be part of it. Let whatever you felt, or didn't feel, land without having to explain it to each other.

Then, later, there is another skill.

How do you talk about what happened without turning it into feedback, fixing, reassurance or "Did I do it right?"

Because being able to feel what happened is one thing.

Being able to put words to it, and really hear what happened for your partner, is another.

If you added The Fifteen Minutes After when you bought The Trace, that's where you go next.

I'll give you a simple structure for that conversation, so you don't have to work out what to say or how to say it.

But not yet.

For now, let The Trace finish here.
